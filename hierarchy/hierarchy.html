Into The Wild


John Krakauer

Jim Gallien



had driven four miles out of


Fairbanks when he spotted the hitchhiker standing in the snow beside the road, thumb raised high, shivering in the gray Alaska

dawn. He didn’t appear to be very old: eighteen, maybe nineteen at most. A rifle protruded


the young man’s backpack, but he looked friendly enough; a hitchhiker

with a Remington semiautomatic isn’t the sort of thing that givesmotorists pause in the forty-ninth state. Gallien steered

his truck onto the

shoulder and told the kid to climb in.

The hitchhiker swung




his pack into the bed of the Ford and introduced

himself as Alex. “Alex?” Gallien

responded, fishing for a last name.


“Just Alex,” the young man replied, pointedly

rejecting the bait. Five feet seven or eight with a

wiry build, he claimed to be twenty-four years old and said he was from South


Dakota. He explained that he wanted a ride as far as the edge of Denali National

Park, where he intended to walk deep into the




bush and “live off the land

for a few months.”



Reflection


Learning Curve

Whilst working on this "arachist" code, I learnt how to think in terms of code, by which I mean that all must be intentional. If you want a space, you must type a code for a space, if you want a line, you must code in a line, etc.

The computer ony knows what you tell it.


Hierarchy

When it comes to the concept of hierarchy, which was implemented prior as an agent of chaos rather than that of structure, I was aware of the idea, but had never really given it much thought, more just casually accepted it as a different size text or bold letters, without percieving there being a ranking or competetion against other text or words on the screen or page.


My use of hierarchy altered emphasis onto previously inconsequential words, forcing what had been meant to be percieved as important, into forgetable text. For example, I shrank down the text of the title to be less than some of the actual passage, and then proceeded to make the first character mentioned in a larger and bolder text than that of the author, causing confusion as to who was truly the author. Random words were made much larger than others, giving them a seemingly more important meaning, though they were in fact not.


Applied Learning

I was able to apply the knowledge of how to code in bold letters, italics, spaces/breaks, horizontal lines, and varying sizes of headers to completely alter the passage into illegible nonsense whilst keeping all of the original text.


(I Can't Get No) Satisfaction

I am somewhat satisfied with my work but would like to be able to alter the text further. I would like to change the way that different lines of the passage were centered on the page, or make them swap back and forth so that every other line is on a different side of the screen, or some be upsidedown, or even slowly falling and piling up on the bottom of the screen, where the user would have to pick them up and try and put it back in the right order.


Designing Information

I can most certainly see the role that a designer would have in the portrayal of information, for there is great power in how information is displayed. The designer chooses importance and even what is remembered, all with the way the piece is displayed. Should a text be made larger, bolder, and centered in comparison to the others, it will be percieved as a piece of vital information, regardless if there is much more important information elsewhere.
